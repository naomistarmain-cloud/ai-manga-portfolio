# Camera Direction

## Principle

Choose camera direction from the emotional and narrative purpose of the CUT, not merely for visual decoration.

## Decision order

1. Define what the audience should notice or feel.
2. Confirm the subject's one primary action.
3. Select a shot size and angle.
4. Add camera movement only when it strengthens the CUT.
5. Confirm that subject motion and camera motion are not both overly complex.
6. Define the opening and final composition.

## Vertical 9:16 rules

- Prioritize extreme close-up, close-up, bust shot, medium shot, and readable vertical full-body shots.
- Keep essential faces, hands, and props away from extreme edges.
- Use depth instead of relying on wide horizontal staging.
- Use foreground framing, vertical architecture, cliffs, stairs, smoke, falling snow, and rising movement effectively.
- Leave reasonable safe space at the top and bottom.

## Camera vocabulary

- Locked shot: quiet tension, observation, subtle change.
- Dolly in: concentration, realization, emotional pressure.
- Dolly out: isolation, revelation of surroundings, emotional distance.
- Fast zoom in: shock or machine reaction; target one clear detail.
- Tracking: walking or running; keep direction simple.
- POV: awakening, discovery, fear; limit gaze movement.
- Tilt up or down: reveal height, scale, or a vertical relationship.
- Orbit: relationship or revelation; do not combine with complicated acting.
- Rack focus: redirect attention between exactly two subjects.

## AI safety

- Do not combine complex body movement, facial acting, object handling, and elaborate camera movement in one CUT.
- Avoid 360-degree orbit during walking, fighting, or character contact.
- Use a stable camera when dialogue or delicate acting is the priority.
- Explicitly state camera direction, speed, target, and stopping point.
